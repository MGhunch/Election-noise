# Sort

**Reads:** the sourced material · **Produces:** size, shape and position

Score a policy three ways. The assessments are walled off from each other — a judgement in one must never leak into another.

Watchouts: structurally huge is not the same as politically loaded · politically hot is not the same as bigger in scale · soon-arriving is not the same as simple mechanism · flagship feels is not the same as structural mechanism.

**Every section needs a "why": five words maximum, naming the mechanism.** Never a hedge, never the party's framing or usual position. The confidence marker already carries the doubt, so the why states the reason plainly.

---

## Size

Three rungs:

- **Flagship** — campaign-defining. Leader's-speech-paragraph-one.
- **Significant** — large reach, operating within the existing system.
- **Niche** — targeted, specific constituency, technical or incremental.

*On the map these read as Broad impact, Key policy and Specific policy. Same three rungs, plainer words.*

### The reach test

**Size measures reach. Not spend, not impact, and not how interesting the mechanism is.**

This is the rule that does the work the three definitions above can't do on their own.

**Universal or cohort is the gate.** A cohort stays Niche however large it is. Free cervical screening reaches roughly 1.4 million people and is Niche, because it reaches one age and sex cohort. Free prescriptions reaches everyone, and is Significant.

**Architecture or operating-inside-the-system is the discriminator** — applied only after the gate, never instead of it. Restructuring the income tax scale is Flagship. Cutting rates within that scale is Significant.

**A small instrument discounts stated reach.** A $50m auditor claiming authority over every public department is still Niche. What the instrument can actually do bounds what it reaches.

**Legal scope is not reach.** A *national* permitted-activity regime for rooftop solar still only reaches homeowners who choose to install solar. Niche.

### The failure mode

Sizing by how structurally interesting the mechanism is. **Novel institutions feel big.** A new authority, a new entity, a new parallel system — these read as major because they're unfamiliar, not because they reach many people.

A party whose platform is largely new institutions will collect an implausible number of Flagships on a first pass. Apply the gate first, every time.

Score what the policy does, not what it's for. Where the money is pointed never changes the size.

## Shape

Two axes, scored independently.

**Immediacy** — when it lands, not how urgent it feels.
1 (within a year) to 5 (generational or undated)

**Mechanism** — what kind of lever it is, not how big.
1 (cash in hand) to 3 (free services) to 5 (structural rewiring)

## Position

Two axes, scored separately. Don't let one pull the other into line.

### Economic

Does the government step in, or step back. Left is redistribution and government ownership. Right is market forces and individual responsibility.

- **1** — a nudge you can feel. Low bar on purpose.
- **2** — clearly moves the settings.
- **3** — rewires. State ownership, universal transfer, privatisation.

Negative steps in, positive steps back. Zero isn't a rung, it's a verdict: the pulls balance (name both in the why), or the axis barely applies. The engaged flag records which, and party averages count engaged records only.

### Direction

New, old, or neither. Not whether it's a good idea.

**First: which is it?**

- **New** — a setting New Zealand has never run.
- **Old** — a return to one we have. Restoring a repealed law is old, whoever passed it.
- **Neither** — 0. Most policy is neither, and 0 means nothing unusual here, not "a bit new."

Free cervical screening, GP loans, faster building consents. None of these wind the clock back or do anything New Zealand hasn't done. They're zeroes.

**Then: how far?** 1 a bit, 2 clearly, 3 as far as it goes. Positive new, negative old.

Capital gains tax is a 2 — taxing property gains isn't new, taxing them with no time limit is.

**The party has to be doing the reaching.** If you had to go digging for the precedent, it isn't one. Rent caps point at 1939 and the Greens mean it — old. Compulsory KiwiSaver points at 1997 and National doesn't — the party is selling a step forward, so score that.

**Don't score the pipe.** A familiar loan scheme or consenting tool is how a policy gets delivered, not where it goes.

Where the destination is genuinely ambiguous, the party's own stated destination places it.

### Both axes

**Score the spine** — the dominant instrument. Name the counter-pull in the why. A balanced zero should be rare.

**Judge against today's settings.** If a Direction call rests on a prior setting the source doesn't mention, name it in the confidence note. That's the checkable claim.

## Confidence

One value for the whole record:

- **High** — the source supported a clear call across every section.
- **Low** — the work was done, but the source was thin or the call was genuinely close. The score stands; hold it loosely.
- **Check** — needs a human look before it's trusted.

If one section is driving a lower mark, name which. That includes the description: a record can be scored confidently on size and shape while the sentence underneath it rests on a costing table.

## Robot proposes, human disposes

These scores are a proposal. Sizes then get calibrated by hand, and the corrections are logged.

On the last full pass the robot was overruled on 35 of 82 records. The pattern, in one line: **the robot sized by how deep the mechanism went, and was corrected to reach.** Of ten records it moved up a rung on rescore, seven moved on nothing — same policy, same numbers, same facts in both descriptions. All seven were reverted.

That gap is why the reach test is written down here, rather than living in a chat log.

Direction went the same way on a later pass. The robot found a precedent for almost everything — 77 records, not one of them scored 0 — because the old low bar invited it. Rescored under the rules above, 26 moved, every one of them upward. That gap is why *ordinary policy scores 0* and *don't score the pipe* are written down too.
